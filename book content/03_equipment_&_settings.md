# Equipment & Settings

A camera records light, but you decide what to show. Understanding your equipment helps you control which parts of a scene appear sharp, how movement looks and how bright the photograph becomes. You can learn these principles with a smartphone, a borrowed camera or a simple camera and lens.

## Cameras

In a digital camera, a **sensor** converts the light projected by the lens into information that becomes an image. A film camera records that light on light-sensitive film instead. The lens, recording surface and exposure controls work together; the camera body alone does not determine the result.

### Main types of cameras

These categories overlap: medium format describes a recording size, for example, while mirrorless describes a camera design.

| Type | Main characteristics | Strengths and limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Smartphone** | Built-in lenses, touchscreen controls and extensive automatic processing; some apps offer manual controls and RAW files. | Convenient for everyday practice. Small sensors and limited optical controls can restrict some effects; portrait-mode blur is often simulated. |
| **Compact / point-and-shoot** | A permanently attached lens, either a fixed focal length or a zoom. Sensor sizes and manual controls vary considerably. | Portable and simple. Advanced models offer substantial control, but the lens cannot be exchanged. |
| **Bridge / superzoom** | A fixed lens with a broad zoom range, usually in a body with a substantial grip and an electronic viewing system. | Covers near and distant subjects without changing lenses. Many use small sensors, which can limit results in low light. |
| **DSLR (digital single-lens reflex)** | A mirror directs light from the lens into an optical viewfinder, then lifts for the exposure. Usually accepts interchangeable lenses. | Direct optical viewing and often good battery endurance. The mirror mechanism adds bulk. |
| **Mirrorless interchangeable-lens camera** | Uses the imaging sensor to provide a live view on a screen or electronic viewfinder; no reflex mirror. | Offers interchangeable lenses and a useful brightness preview. Continuous electronic viewing consumes battery power; not every model has a viewfinder. |
| **Action / rugged camera** | Small, often with a fixed wide-angle lens and a protective or waterproof design. | Useful for activities and difficult conditions. Check the actual water-depth rating and available photographic controls. |
| **Film camera** | Uses film rather than a digital sensor. Includes compact, SLR, rangefinder and larger-format designs. Instant cameras produce a physical print using compatible instant film. | Teaches the same exposure principles, with ongoing film and processing costs and less immediate feedback. |
| **Medium-format digital camera / large-format film camera** | Uses a recording area larger than full frame; large-format cameras commonly use individual sheets of film. | Offers specialised image-making possibilities, often with greater cost, size and a slower workflow. |

For the DSLR and mirrorless designs, see [Canon's comparison](https://www.canon.com.au/get-inspired/mirrorless-or-dslr-cameras). [Canon's compact-camera overview](https://www.canon.com.au/compact-cameras) illustrates how widely fixed-lens cameras vary, while [ILFORD's film guide](https://www.ilfordphoto.com/wp/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Ilford-Product-Brochure-LOW-RES-WEB-1.pdf) explains common film formats.

### Specifications that matter

**Sensor size** is the sensor's physical area, not its megapixel count. Common formats include Micro Four Thirds (about 17.3 × 13 mm), APS-C (roughly 24 × 16 mm, varying by manufacturer) and full frame (about 36 × 24 mm). Phones and compacts use several smaller formats; some compacts also use APS-C or full-frame sensors. Digital medium format is larger than full frame, with dimensions varying by system.

Larger sensors can offer advantages in low light and allow shallower depth of field at the same framing and f-number. Smaller formats can support lighter systems. Results also depend on sensor technology, the lens and technique. **Resolution**, expressed in megapixels (millions of pixels), affects potential detail, cropping and print size. More megapixels also mean larger files; they cannot repair missed focus or motion blur. See [Hasselblad's sensor-size explanation](https://store-na.hasselblad.com/blogs/camera-basics/camera-sensor-sizes-explained).

Other specifications help answer practical questions:

- **Lens mount:** Which lenses fit, and which functions work? Check both physical and electronic compatibility.
- **Autofocus:** Can it follow your subject reliably, including in the light you expect to use?
- **Burst rate and buffer:** How many frames per second can it take, and for how long before slowing down?
- **Stabilisation:** Does the lens or body compensate for camera shake? This helps with stationary subjects at slower shutter speeds, but does not stop subject movement. See [Canon's explanation of stabilisation](https://files.canon-europe.com/files/webcontent/rf-lens-world/knowledge/correction/index.html).
- **Handling and endurance:** Can you comfortably reach the controls, see the display and carry the camera? Consider battery life and weather protection as well.
- **File formats:** JPEG files are processed and ready to share. RAW files retain more capture information for editing, but need compatible software and more storage. RAW+JPEG records both, where available. See [Nikon's RAW and JPEG guide](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/is-shooting-raw-or-jpeg-better-for-you).

## Lenses

A photographic lens gathers light and forms a focused image on the sensor or film. Its optical design, focal length and aperture influence how much of the scene fits in the frame, how large the subject appears and how the sharp and blurred areas look.

### Focal length and lens types

**Focal length**, measured in millimetres (mm), describes an optical property rather than the physical length of the lens. On the same camera, shorter focal lengths show a wider view; longer ones show a narrower view and make a distant subject larger in the frame.

| Lens category | Approximate full-frame focal lengths | Common uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wide-angle** | Below 35 mm | Interiors, landscapes and showing a subject in its surroundings. |
| **Standard** | About 35–60 mm | Everyday scenes and general photography. |
| **Telephoto** | About 70 mm and longer | Portraits, distant details, sport and wildlife. |
| **Macro** | Various focal lengths | Small subjects at close range. A 1:1 reproduction ratio means the image on the sensor is the same size as the subject itself. |

A **prime** has one focal length; a **zoom** offers a range. Primes often offer a wide maximum aperture in a compact package. Zooms make it easy to change framing without changing lenses. Macro capability is a separate feature: a telephoto lens does not necessarily focus close enough for life-size photographs. See [Nikon's focal-length guide](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-focal-length?generate=pdf).

Sensor size also affects the view. Multiply focal length by the camera's **crop factor** to compare its angle of view with full frame: a 35 mm lens on a 1.6× APS-C camera gives a view similar to 56 mm on full frame. The lens remains a 35 mm lens. This comparison does not change its marked aperture or turn it into a different lens. See [Canon's explanation of crop factor](https://www.canon.ca/en/Articles/2023/Full-Frame-Camera-Benefits).

### Parts and features of a lens

| Part or feature | Purpose and effect on the photograph |
| --- | --- |
| **Optical elements and groups** | Individual pieces of optical material work together to form the image and correct imperfections. More elements do not automatically mean better quality. |
| **Aspherical and low-dispersion elements** | Special shapes and materials reduce optical defects, helping sharpness and reducing coloured fringes around edges. |
| **Surface coatings** | Reduce internal reflections, helping preserve contrast and limit flare and ghost images around bright lights. |
| **Aperture diaphragm and blades** | Form an adjustable opening that controls light and depth of field. Blade shape also influences the appearance of out-of-focus highlights. |
| **Focusing group, motor and focus ring** | Change the distance at which subjects appear sharp. Motor performance affects autofocus speed and noise; the ring provides manual control where supported. |
| **Zoom mechanism and ring** | Change focal length on a zoom lens, altering the view and subject size in the frame. |
| **Optical stabiliser, if fitted** | Moves an internal group to counter camera shake. It cannot freeze a moving person. |
| **Mount and contacts** | Connect the lens to the camera and, where supported, carry power and control information. An incompatible combination may lose autofocus or aperture control. |
| **Filter thread and hood fitting** | Accept correctly sized accessories. A hood blocks stray light; filters can alter light before it enters the lens. |

The optical explanations draw on [Canon's lens-technology guide](https://files.canon-europe.com/files/webcontent/rf-lens-world/features/technology/index.html).

A label such as **18–55 mm f/3.5–5.6** describes the zoom range and the widest available aperture at its ends: f/3.5 at 18 mm and f/5.6 at 55 mm. It can usually also use smaller openings, such as f/8. A **50 mm f/1.8** is a prime with a maximum aperture of f/1.8. Check minimum focusing distance, sensor coverage and mount compatibility too. [Nikon explains these aperture markings](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-maximum-aperture).

## Getting started

The minimum equipment is **a working camera with a lens, power and somewhere to record photographs**. A smartphone already combines these. A digital camera generally needs a charged battery and a compatible memory card; a film camera needs compatible film and a way to develop it.

For learning, begin with equipment you already have or can borrow. If you choose an interchangeable-lens camera, one general-purpose lens is enough. The zoom supplied in a camera kit lets you explore several focal lengths before deciding whether you need anything else. A tripod, flash and second lens can wait until a particular photograph calls for them.

Before your first outing:

1. Charge the battery and check available storage.
2. Clean the front of the lens with suitable lens-cleaning materials.
3. Find the shutter button, playback, focus controls and brightness adjustment in the manual or camera app.
4. Attach a strap if using a separate camera, and take a test photograph.
5. Check that you can view the image and copy it somewhere safe. Keep a second copy of photographs you value before deleting the originals.

Start in automatic mode if that helps you concentrate on the scene. Photograph one subject from several positions, then compare the results. Add manual control gradually as you learn what you want to change.

## Camera settings

**Aperture, shutter speed and ISO** work together to determine image brightness. Aperture and shutter speed control the light recorded; digital ISO affects how that signal is converted into image brightness. **Focus** determines the distance that appears sharp. A bright enough photograph can still be blurred.

### Aperture: opening and depth of field

Aperture is expressed as an **f-number**, such as f/2.8, f/5.6 or f/11. A lower number means a larger opening. With other settings unchanged, a larger opening admits more light.

**Depth of field** is the range of distances that appears acceptably sharp. At the same focal length and focus distance, a wide aperture such as f/2.8 gives less depth of field than f/8. Use a wider opening to help separate a person from the background; try f/8 for a scene needing more detail from front to back. Neither setting guarantees the result: focus placement and distances matter too. See [Canon's depth-of-field guide](https://files.canon-europe.com/files/webcontent/rf-lens-world/knowledge/depth-of-field/index.html).

Very small openings, such as f/22, can soften fine detail through **diffraction**, so the largest f-number is not automatically the sharpest choice. See [Georgia State University's explanation](https://www.hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/phyopt/diflim.html).

Many phone lenses have a fixed physical aperture. A portrait-mode “aperture” slider may change simulated background blur instead.

### Shutter speed: time and movement

Shutter speed is the duration of the exposure, measured in seconds or fractions of a second. **1/1000 s is shorter and faster than 1/60 s.** A shorter exposure lets in less light and can freeze movement; a longer exposure lets in more light and can record blur.

These are starting points to test, rather than guaranteed settings:

| Situation | Shutter speed to try | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person standing fairly still | 1/125–1/250 s | Is the face sharp when enlarged? |
| Running person or active animal | 1/500–1/2000 s | Faster movement and closer subjects may need a shorter exposure. |
| Flowing water with visible blur | 1/4–2 s | Use a stable support and check brightness. |
| Night scene with light trails | Several seconds | Keep the camera still and inspect bright areas for lost detail. |

Blur can come from **camera shake**, **subject movement** or **missed focus**. A tripod addresses camera movement, while a faster shutter helps freeze subject movement. Refocusing addresses a different problem. See [Nikon's shutter-speed guide](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-shutter-speed).

### ISO: brightness and image quality

Digital ISO describes the camera's exposure index and associated signal handling. Raising it generally makes the image brighter at the same aperture and shutter speed; it does **not** gather more light. ISO 800 produces roughly twice the brightness of ISO 400 under otherwise identical conditions.

Low ISO settings are useful when there is enough light or a stationary scene allows a longer exposure. Higher settings help maintain a fast shutter in dim light. Such photographs often show more **noise**—visible speckles or grain-like texture—and less flexibility in very bright areas. Much of the noise comes from capturing less light, rather than ISO alone.

Use the lowest ISO that still allows the shutter speed and aperture your photograph needs. A little noise is often preferable to unwanted motion blur. **Auto ISO** can adjust ISO while you concentrate on the other controls. See [Nikon's ISO guide](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-iso-sensitivity).

### Balancing the three controls

A **stop** is a doubling or halving of light or, for ISO, the corresponding brightness setting. At a fixed ISO, f/4 at 1/250 s gives approximately the same brightness as f/5.6 at 1/125 s: the smaller opening halves the light per unit time, while the longer exposure doubles the time. Depth of field and motion blur can still change.

Common modes divide the decisions between you and the camera:

| Mode | What you choose |
| --- | --- |
| **Auto** | The camera handles most decisions. |
| **Program (P)** | The camera selects aperture and shutter speed; you retain other controls, depending on the model. |
| **Aperture priority (A / Av)** | You choose aperture; the camera selects shutter speed. |
| **Shutter priority (S / Tv)** | You choose shutter speed; the camera selects aperture. |
| **Manual (M)** | You choose both aperture and shutter speed. Auto ISO, if enabled, may still change brightness. |

In automatic and priority modes, **exposure compensation** asks for a brighter (+) or darker (−) result. It is useful when the camera's meter misjudges a very light or dark scene. Check the photograph as well as the meter: the intended mood matters. These controls are introduced in [Nikon's exposure overview](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/a-basic-look-at-the-basics-of-exposure) and the [Sydney Art School handbook](https://sydneyartschool.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Photography-Handbook-v1.0-Module-A-Camera-Basics-B-The-Photoshoot-compressed.pdf).

### Focus: choosing what matters

Autofocus decides where to place sharpness, but you can guide it:

- **Single autofocus (often AF-S or One-Shot):** focuses once when activated; useful for stationary subjects.
- **Continuous autofocus (often AF-C or Servo):** keeps adjusting while activated; useful when the subject's distance changes.
- **Focus area:** a single point gives precise placement; tracking or subject detection can help follow a moving subject. For portraits, check that the intended eye is selected.
- **Manual focus (MF):** lets you adjust focus yourself. Magnified live view helps with precise work, especially when autofocus struggles.

On many cameras, half-pressing the shutter activates autofocus; on a phone, tap the subject. Low light, reflections and plain surfaces can confuse autofocus. Try a detailed edge at the same distance and inspect the result. Names and behaviour vary by model; see [Nikon School's autofocus guide](https://dev.nikonschool.co.uk/hints-and-tips/your-essential-guide-to-nikon-autofocus).

**Practice:** Photograph a stationary object with a background several steps behind it. In aperture priority, compare the widest available aperture with f/8, keeping your position and focus point unchanged. Next, use shutter priority to photograph movement at 1/1000 s and 1/30 s, allowing ISO to adjust if needed. Enlarge the photographs and identify what changed: background detail, movement, brightness or noise.

## Other accessories

Add accessories when they solve a problem you have encountered.

| Accessory | Why it can be useful |
| --- | --- |
| **Strap and padded bag** | Make carrying equipment easier and reduce the risk of drops and knocks. |
| **Spare battery and compatible charger** | Extend shooting time away from power. |
| **Spare memory card and card reader** | Provide recording capacity and a convenient way to transfer files. Check compatibility. |
| **Tripod and suitable head** | Hold the camera steady for long exposures, close-ups or repeated framing. A phone needs a suitable clamp. |
| **Remote release** | Triggers the camera without touching it. A self-timer can often serve the same purpose. |
| **Lens hood** | Blocks stray light that can reduce contrast; must match the lens to avoid dark corners. |
| **Hand blower and lens cloth** | Remove loose dust and fingerprints from suitable surfaces. Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions. |
| **Reflector** | Bounces existing light onto a subject to brighten shadows. White card can work for small subjects. |
| **External flash or LED light** | Adds light when existing illumination is insufficient or poorly placed. Bouncing or diffusing it can soften shadows. |
| **Circular polarising filter** | Reduces some reflections from water and glass and can deepen blue skies; the effect depends on angle. |
| **Neutral-density (ND) filter** | Reduces incoming light, allowing longer exposures or wider apertures in bright conditions. |
| **Rain cover** | Adds protection outdoors; check the equipment's limits rather than assuming weather sealing means waterproofing. |
| **Backup storage** | An external drive or suitable cloud service provides an additional copy of important photographs. |

For equipment and lighting basics, see the [Sydney Art School handbook](https://sydneyartschool.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Photography-Handbook-v1.0-Module-A-Camera-Basics-B-The-Photoshoot-compressed.pdf). [Canon's filter guide](https://www.canon.com.hk/en/club/article/itemDetail.do?itemId=10355) explains the different effects of polarising and neutral-density filters.
